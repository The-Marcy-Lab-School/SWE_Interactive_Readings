What Is a Program, Really?

~11 minutes Interactive No coding experience needed

1. The book that cost more than a house

In 2011, an out-of-print biology book called The Making of a Fly was listed on Amazon by two used-book sellers, profnath and bordeebook. Each ran a simple pricing program: profnath set its price to 0.9983 times bordeebook's current price, always slightly cheaper; bordeebook set its price to 1.270589 times profnath's, always pricier. Neither program had a bug — each did exactly what it was told, once a day, using whatever price the other had just posted.

That is a loop with no exit condition. Each output became the other's next input, growing a little bigger every round, until the price hit $23,698,655.93 on April 18th. A person finally noticed and reset profnath's price by hand, and bordeebook's program obediently multiplied that new price too. The missing piece was never "multiply by this number"; it was "stop if the result looks ridiculous," an instruction nobody thought to write. That single gap — a computer doing exactly what its instructions say with zero judgment about whether the result makes sense — is the foundation everything else in programming sits on top of.

Source: the full price log survives; biologist Michael Eisen first spotted the listing and wrote up exactly what he found.

3. Instructions run in order, exactly as written

Two things are true of every program: instructions run in the exact order they're written, and each one runs exactly as written, with no pausing to guess your intent. Swap two steps and you often get a broken result, even though each instruction is still correct alone. Leave a step vague, or skip one the computer needs, and nothing quietly patches the gap. A person fills in a vague recipe with common sense without noticing; a computer has no such fallback.

The classic way to see this is with a task everyone already knows how to do: making a peanut butter sandwich. Compare a vague version of the instructions against an exact one.

Notice "put peanut butter on" isn't a real instruction: on what, with what, how much? A human fills in "the bread, a knife, a reasonable amount" without noticing the gap; a computer has nothing to fill it with unless someone wrote that down. It's the same gap that sent profnath and bordeebook's prices to $23 million: "multiply by this number" is exact on its own, but "stop if this looks ridiculous" never got written down.

Try it: here's a different everyday task, broken into steps but shuffled out of order. Put them back in the order a person (or a program) actually has to perform them.

  • Fill the kettle with cold water
  • Turn on the kettle and wait for the water to boil
  • Put a tea bag in the mug
  • Pour the boiling water into the mug
  • Wait 3-4 minutes, then take the tea bag out
Need a hint?

Nothing can boil before there's water in the kettle, and nothing can be poured before it's finished boiling. The tea bag can go in the mug any time before the pour, but it can't do anything on its own after that except sit and steep.

6. Summary & reflection

Two sellers' pricing programs multiplied a number back and forth until a used book hit $23.7 million, not because either program had a bug, but because "stop if this gets ridiculous" was never written down as an instruction. Every program, from that pricing bot to the first line of Python this fellowship writes, works the same way: instructions run in the exact order they're written, each one exactly as written, with no common-sense fallback for anything left vague or missing.
